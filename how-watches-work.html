Movement guide

How a mechanical watch works

Follow the energy from your fingertips on the crown all the way to the sweep of the seconds hand. No engineering degree required.

The big picture

Store energy, then release it evenly

Every mechanical watch solves the same problem: how to take the burst of energy you put in when you wind it and let it out slowly, in perfectly equal amounts, for a day or more.

The solution has four stages: a power source (the mainspring), a transmission (the gear train), a regulator (the escapement and balance) and a display (the hands). Understanding these four makes every other part of watchmaking easier to follow.

Step by step

The journey of energy

  1. Winding the crown. Turning the crown drives the crown wheel, which meshes with the ratchet wheel on top of the barrel. In an automatic watch, a rotor and reverser wheels do this job as your wrist moves.
  2. Tightening the mainspring. The ratchet wheel turns the barrel arbor, coiling the mainspring tighter around it. The click, a sprung pawl, catches each tooth so the spring cannot unwind backwards.
  3. Releasing through the barrel. The spring tries to uncoil, turning the toothed barrel slowly. Its outer teeth drive the first wheel of the gear train.
  4. Gearing up. The centre wheel, third wheel and fourth wheel multiply the rotation. The fourth wheel typically turns once a minute and often carries the seconds hand.
  5. The escapement. The escape wheel tries to spin, but the pallet fork locks and unlocks it in tiny steps. Each unlock gives the balance a small push.
  6. The balance keeps time. The balance wheel and hairspring swing back and forth at a constant rate, often 4 times a second (28,800 vibrations per hour). Each swing lets the escapement advance one step.
  7. The hands move. Through the motion works, a small set of gears under the dial, the minute and hour hands turn at exactly the right speed.
Spotlight

The ratchet wheel and click

Look through the case back of many watches and you will see a large, often decorated wheel near the crown. That is usually the ratchet wheel. It is fixed to the barrel arbor, so turning it winds the mainspring.

Next to it sits the click, a small lever pressed against the teeth by a spring. As you wind, you may hear it clicking. Its job is simple and vital: let the ratchet wheel turn in the winding direction, and block it from turning back.

Watchmakers often finish the ratchet wheel with sunburst or snailing patterns, which is why it is one of the most admired parts of a visible movement.

Accuracy

What affects timekeeping

PositionGravity pulls on the balance differently when the watch is face-up, crown-down and so on.Note how you rest it overnight; small changes can offset gains or losses.
TemperatureHeat and cold slightly change the hairspring’s elasticity.Modern alloys reduce this; avoid leaving watches in hot cars.
MagnetismA magnetised hairspring coils stick together and the watch runs very fast.Keep away from strong magnets; a watchmaker can demagnetise it.
State of windA nearly unwound mainspring delivers less consistent power.Keep the watch well wound, especially near the end of its reserve.
Lubrication ageOils thicken over years, increasing friction.Regular servicing as recommended by the maker.
A little history

From pocket to wrist

Portable spring-driven timepieces appeared in Europe in the 1500s. For centuries they lived in pockets, protected by hinged covers. The balance spring, introduced in the late 1600s, dramatically improved accuracy.

Wristwatches became widespread in the early twentieth century, when practicality during travel, sport and work made a watch on the wrist far easier to read than one in a pocket. Self-winding mechanisms, water-resistant cases and shock protection followed, and the basic layout of the mechanical movement has remained remarkably similar ever since.
